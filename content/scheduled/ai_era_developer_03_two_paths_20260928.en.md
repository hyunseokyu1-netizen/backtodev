# A Developer's Place in the AI Era (3/5): The Two Forks Ahead, and the FDE Standing Between Them

In [Part 2](/posts/ai_era_developer_02_job_landscape_20260928), I wrote that the three categories sit at completely different maturity levels. Only FDE has hardened into a job, AI Native Engineer is the developer's new baseline, and GTM Owner is still just a role concept.

But looking at this only as "which one's hotter" misses what matters. **AI Native Engineer and GTM Owner aren't competitors — they're opposite directions.**

| | Direction |
|---|---|
| **AI Native Engineer** | **Goes back deeper into technology** |
| **GTM Owner** | **Takes development experience out into business** |

It's not a single seat you're weighing — **it's a fork in the road, deciding which way to walk.** This part is about those two directions, and about the FDE straddling the space between them.

---

## 1. The Two Forks, Drawn Out

Here's how I see it laid out:

```
Developer
  │
  ├── AI Native Engineer        ← deepening into tech
  │       ↓
  │   AI Engineer
  │       ↓
  │   AI Architect
  │       ↓
  │   AI Tech Lead
  │
  └── PM / Technical PM         ← widening into business
          ↓
      GTM Owner
          ↓
      Product / Business Lead
          ↓
      Head of AI Business
```

The left side is **the path that gets deeper.** The difficulty of the technology you handle climbs, and the scope of your decisions widens out to the entire system. The right side is **the path that gets wider.** Starting from technology, responsibility shifts toward customers, pricing, and revenue.

Neither one is the superior path. **There's a top-level seat on both sides.** The top of the left side is the organization's highest technical decision-maker; the top of the right side is the business owner.

---

## 2. Give Them the Same Problem and They Do Completely Different Work

Drawing the distinction in words alone stays fuzzy, so let's hand both the same task.

> **"Let's automate our customer service with AI."**

### What the AI Native Engineer Does

This person **builds.**

- Wiring LLMs to APIs
- Building RAG
- Designing agents
- MCP / tool integration
- Data pipelines
- Connecting to existing system APIs
- Automating AI workflows
- Deployment and monitoring

This is slightly different from just "AI developer." **They're not using a model — they're building a system with a model inside it.** Of everything on that list, the part that actually eats time isn't calling the model — it's **integrating with existing systems and monitoring.** So this is closer to "an engineer who solves problems and builds products using AI."

### What the GTM Owner Does

Same AI customer-service product, and this person **sells.**

- Whether to target finance, manufacturing, or small businesses
- What price to sell it at
- Which features to ship first
- How to acquire customers
- How to work with sales and marketing teams
- How to actually generate revenue

**Separate from whether the product is finished, they're responsible for whether it makes money.** So a GTM Owner can end up closer to business than even a product manager is.

---

## 3. The Two Directions, Side by Side

| Category | AI Native Engineer | GTM Owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Someone who builds products/systems using AI | Someone who sells and grows the product in the market |
| **Central question** | **"How do we build this with AI?"** | **"Who do we sell this to, and how do we spread it?"** |
| **Work** | AI agents, RAG, APIs, automation, product development | Customer discovery, market strategy, pricing, sales, partnerships, launch |
| **Technical weight** | ★★★★★ | ★★ ~ ★★★ |
| **Business weight** | ★★ | ★★★★★ |
| **Coding** | Needed substantially | Needed situationally |
| **Customer contact** | Medium to low | **Very high** |
| **Success metric** | Shipping, technical performance, automation | **Revenue, customer count, conversion rate, market share** |
| **Career direction** | AI Engineer / Solutions Engineer / AI Architect | Product / Growth / Business / Revenue leadership |

The most important row in this table is **success metric.**

The left side **gets judged on what got built.** Did it ship, does it perform, did it get automated. The right side **gets judged on what got sold.** Revenue, customer count, conversion rate.

That difference changes your whole sense of the work. Build something well and it doesn't sell — the left side feels wronged, the right side has failed. **Which side's "feeling wronged" you can tolerate is the real decision criterion, I think.**

And look at the customer-contact row too. The right side is very high. If you hate constantly meeting people, the right side will wear you down. Flip it around — if sitting alone staring at code all day frustrates you, the left side will wear you down. **This isn't a competence question. It's a temperament question.**

---

## 4. The FDE Straddles the Gap Between Them

This is where it clicks why FDE is so hot right now. **It's because it straddles the gap between the two paths.**

```
AI Native Engineer
        ↕
       FDE
        ↕
  Technical PM
        ↕
   GTM Owner
```

An FDE handles technology, customers, and business at the same time. They go on-site to find the problem, build it themselves, get it adopted, and watch whether it turns into actual results. **One person does both the left-side work and the right-side work.**

That produces a few characteristics.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Open in both directions** | You can go from FDE further into tech, or out into business |
| **Neither side goes fully deep** | If you want pure research or pure sales, it can feel frustrating |
| **People who sit at the boundary have the advantage** | Someone who only developed, or only managed, each has half to learn fresh |
| **The definition wobbles company to company** | Some companies emphasize building, others emphasize diagnosis first — worth confirming in the interview |

The third row is my own case. I developed, then took on a team and managed, then came back to building things myself. For a while that history felt like **it was ambiguous either way** — I'd written less code than someone who only developed, and seen less of the org than someone who only managed.

But against a seat like FDE, that ambiguity becomes a qualification. **Finding the problem comes from management experience; building it comes from development experience.** In a seat that needs both, someone who's only deep on one side ends up having to learn the other half from scratch anyway.

Granted, this is a reading colored by my own situation. But job postings actually ask for that exact combination, so it doesn't feel like pure wishful thinking on my part.

---

## 5. So What Do You Decide First

Knowing the two forks doesn't mean you can pick immediately. Here's the order I'm thinking through it in.

1. **Being AI-native isn't optional.** As I wrote in Part 2, it's become the developer baseline. Whether you're headed left or right, you have to pass through here
2. **Check which success metric you can live with.** Whether you can accept "didn't sell" being scored as your own failure is the fork
3. **Check how much customer contact you can tolerate.** Not a competence question — a temperament one. Force it and it won't last
4. **If you can't decide, stand at the boundary.** A seat like FDE that does both will teach your gut which side fits, after one lap around

Option 4 is close to my own choice right now. **When you're not sure which side you are, the seat that does both gives you the most information.**

---

## 6. Wrap-up

1. **AI Native Engineer and GTM Owner aren't competing — they're opposite directions.** One deepens, one widens
2. **Give them the same task and one builds, one sells.** Take the AI customer-service example and the actual work doesn't overlap at all
3. **The decisive difference is the success metric.** Judged on what got built, or judged on what got sold
4. **How much customer contact you can tolerate is a temperament question, not a competence one**
5. **FDE straddles the gap between the two paths.** That's why it's open in both directions, and why neither side goes fully deep
6. **A history that sat at the boundary becomes a qualification for a seat that sits at the boundary**

So why are companies increasingly looking for the right-side person — the one who owns the business — these days? Org-trend explanations alone fall short.

**Part 4 covers the economic reason.** AI products carry a cost structure that old software never had, and because of it, technical decisions have turned directly into P&L decisions.
