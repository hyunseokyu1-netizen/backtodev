# A Developer's Place in the AI Era (5/5): The Scope of Your Curiosity Becomes the Scope of Your Ability

In [Part 1](/posts/ai_era_developer_01_dogfooding_20260928), I wrote that it comes down to problem-solving in the end. In [Part 2](/posts/ai_era_developer_02_job_landscape_20260928), that the three categories sit at completely different maturity levels; in [Part 3](/posts/ai_era_developer_03_two_paths_20260928), that there's a fork between going deeper into tech and widening into business. In [Part 4](/posts/ai_era_developer_04_token_economy_20260928), that tokens becoming a cost line turned technical decisions into P&L decisions.

Which leaves one last question. **What determines whether someone can become that person?**

It's not years of experience — almost nowhere I interviewed asked about it directly. Not a degree, not the number of languages you can use. My conclusion is this:

> **Going forward, the range of a person's ability ends up nearly identical to the range of what they were curious about.**

---

## 1. The Bottleneck Moved

It used to be expensive to even check on something you were curious about.

| | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Curious about a new technology | Buy a book, find examples, days to set up the environment | Ask a question, get it running within the hour |
| Curious about another field | Had to go find someone in that field | Get an overview explained immediately, just check what's wrong |
| Want to build something | A month of weekends | A day of weekend |
| Hit a wall | Stuck, and that's the end | Explain where you're stuck, get the next move |

What matters is the result of this shift. **We moved from an era where "can I do this" was the bottleneck, to an era where "did it occur to me to do this" is the bottleneck.**

When execution was expensive, execution ability was the skill. Once execution gets cheap, execution ability becomes the baseline. Then there's only one difference left: **whether it occurred to you to try it in the first place.**

---

## 2. AI Never Tells You What You Didn't Ask

This is the most practical reason.

AI is a function of the question. It only answers within the scope you throw at it. It answers very intelligently, but **it says nothing at all about something whose existence you're not aware of.**

Two people using the same tool diverge like this:

| | What they asked | What they got |
|---|---|---|
| A | "Fix this code's error" | Fixed code |
| B | "Fix this code's error" → "why does this structure cause this error?" → "then does every other place using this pattern have the same problem?" | Fixed code + the cause + **other spots with the same problem** |

Both use the same model, spend the same time. The entire difference in outcome comes purely from **whether the third question occurred to them.**

So it feels like the skill gap is widening faster than before these days. The tool was handed out equally; **the range of questions was not.**

---

## 3. Why Going Wide Wasn't Wasted

In Part 1, I told the hammer story — a nail needs driving, and time was spent building hammers instead. So was trying all those harness-this, loop-that things a waste?

**No. And this distinction is the most important part of this post.**

Having tried all of it is exactly what gave me standing to say **"that's not actually the core thing."** Say that without having tried it, and it's just an excuse from someone who's fallen behind. Say it after trying it, and it becomes a judgment.

It showed up in interviews too. When I voiced a skeptical opinion about some new concept, the reaction was good — not because my opinion was special, but because **I could say concretely that I'd actually tried it and where exactly I hit a wall.** Naming where you stopped is experience. Saying you never tried is just an attitude.

The point is this: **since the cost of widening your curiosity got cheap, going wide pays off far more than it used to.** Glancing sideways used to be a luxury. Now, glancing sideways is a strategy.

Even the resume management service I built didn't start from anything grand. It was one line: **"why am I still managing where I applied with a spreadsheet?"** Without that question, there's no service, no dogfooding experience from Part 1, and no this series.

---

## 4. Curiosity and Being Scattered Are Different Things

Here's where caution is needed. Curiosity turning into ability comes with a condition. Just poking at things everywhere is being scattered, and that isn't ability.

| Curiosity that becomes ability | Curiosity that doesn't |
|---|---|
| Takes it to a finish at least once (even if small) | Starts keep piling up without finishing |
| Leaves one line about why it didn't work | Drops it the moment it stops being fun |
| Shows it to others and gets judged | Ends with only yourself knowing |
| **Has gone deep on at least one thing** | Only ever skims the surface |
| Curiosity points toward "the nail" | Keeps falling into tools for tools |

**The last row connects back to the hammer story from Part 1.** Curiosity by itself isn't automatically good. **It only becomes ability when it points at a real problem.** Getting absorbed in polishing a tool is also curiosity, but from a company's perspective, that's a cost.

And the fourth row matters too. For breadth to have value, **you need to have experienced depth at least once.** Dig one thing all the way down, and you learn what's actually there when you go all the way down. With that sense, skimming other fields lightly still lets you guess where the real difficulty is. Without that experience, every field looks easy.

---

## 5. How to Widen the Range of Your Curiosity

Ending on something abstract wouldn't be useful, so here's what I'm actually doing.

1. **Write down "why is this set up this way?" the moment it occurs to me.** Finding the answer can come later. What matters is not letting the question slip away. A moment that felt odd disappears if you don't catch it within a day
2. **Build the smallest possible version.** The goal isn't completion — it's finding out "where I get stuck." Where you get stuck is that field's real difficulty
3. **Leave one line about where I got stuck.** This becomes the basis for judgment later. "I tried it, and this is where I stopped" is a powerful sentence in an interview or a meeting
4. **Show it to someone.** As I said in Part 1, getting judged outside is part of the same cycle
5. **Keep digging into one thing continuously.** Breadth alone has no grounding

One more thing on top of this. **Write about it.** This blog is my record of curiosity. Post count isn't proof of skill, but **how many distinct topics you've touched shows the range of your curiosity directly.** And that record is actually used later when someone else judges you.

---

## 6. So Is the Foundation Unnecessary Now?

This objection always comes up when I say curiosity is everything. If someone who knows nothing just has curiosity, is that enough? It's a fair point, and here's how I see it.

**Fundamentals determine the resolution of your questions.**

Looking at the same screen, the questions you're able to ask differ.

| Without fundamentals | With fundamentals |
|---|---|
| "It's slow" | "Is the first response slow, or is the whole thing slow?" |
| "There's an error" | "Did the request not go out, or did it go out and come back wrong?" |
| "The AI's answering weird" | "Does it answer differently every time for the same input?" |
| "The bill's high" | "Did the request count go up, or did the size per request go up?" |

Ask the right-column question and the answer comes back in one shot. Ask the left-column one and you need five more rounds of conversation. **Fundamentals haven't lost their value in the AI era — their role shifted from "solving it yourself directly" to "asking precisely."**

So the order ends up like this. Fundamentals build the resolution of a question; curiosity builds its range. **Range without resolution is shallow; resolution without range is narrow.** Both are needed, but where resolution used to eat all your time, now there's time freed up for range.

---

## 7. Curiosity Shows Up Better Than Expected in Interviews

Interviewing multiple times this round, I learned that companies don't ask about curiosity directly. Instead they check through questions like:

- What's something you recently learned that stuck with you
- Is there anything you did that nobody asked you to do
- Is there anything that felt off while using our product
- What made you notice that problem

The last one especially. **"How did you solve it" is a question about execution ability; "how did you discover it" is a question about curiosity.** Tell the same story, and people who can explain the discovery process diverge from people who can't.

And once the third question comes up, it's nearly certain: **they're checking whether the candidate was curious enough to try the company's product beforehand.** Naming one concrete piece of friction right there is itself the answer. It carries weight not as a sign you prepared hard, but as a sign you tinkered with it out of genuine curiosity.

On the flip side, this kind of answer didn't land well. When asked what you recently learned, **just listing concept names.** Everyone knows names. It only sounds different once it's paired with "and I actually tried it myself."

I'd rewrite the claim that "AI replaces developers" like this:

> **AI replaces "work you do because you were told to." Deciding what to tell it to do is what's left. And what you decide to tell it is decided by what you were curious about.**

Knowledge is nearly free now. Execution got a lot cheaper too. The scarce resource left is **how many times you ask "why is this set up this way?"**

I think that's going to be the ceiling on a person's ability.
